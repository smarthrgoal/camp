# Day 2: Understanding the User and Discovering Their Needs

## 1. Day Overview

Day 2 takes teams from simply identifying a problem to understanding the people who experience it. Students learn that an idea becomes valuable only when it addresses a real user need.

Through perspective-taking activities, role-play interviews, and evidence-sorting exercises, students identify the primary user, distinguish observations from assumptions, and develop an evidence-based **User Need Card**.

### Essential Question

**How can we understand what people really need?**

### Day Purpose

Students learn to design for real people rather than relying on their own assumptions. They identify users, needs, frustrations, goals, and unanswered questions that can guide the development of a useful solution.

---

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the day, students will be able to:

1. Identify the primary user who is most directly affected by the selected problem.
2. Distinguish between an observation, an assumption, evidence, and a question.
3. Use open-ended questions to explore a user's experience through a short role-play interview.
4. Write a user need statement supported by at least two pieces of evidence.

# 4. User-Centred Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is user-centred when a team begins with the people affected by a problem and learns from their experiences before creating a solution.

A team may identify a real problem on Day 1, but it still needs to understand:

* Who experiences the problem?
* What is the person trying to achieve?
* What makes the current experience difficult?
* What does the person currently do to manage the situation?

### Key Principle

**Do not design based only on the team's assumptions. Learn from the people who actually experience the problem.**

## 4.1 The General Public Is Not the Same as the User

| Term               | Meaning                                                                                        | Example                                                           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **General Public** | A broad population that is too large to guide one focused solution.                            | Everyone in the school or everyone who uses the internet.         |
| **User Group**     | People who share a relevant situation, goal, or difficulty.                                    | Students trying to join school clubs before registration closes.  |
| **Specific User**  | A clearly defined segment within the user group that experiences the problem in a similar way. | New students who do not know where club information is published. |

## 4.2 Primary, Secondary, and Influencing Users

| User Type              | Role in the Experience                                                           | Guiding Question                     | Example                                                     |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary User**       | Experiences the problem or will use the future solution most directly.           | Who is most directly affected?       | A student trying to find a club and register.               |
| **Secondary User**     | Uses, supports, or is affected by part of the experience.                        | Who else interacts with the process? | A student club leader receiving registrations.              |
| **Influencing Person** | Influences access, decisions, rules, or information without being the main user. | Who influences what the user can do? | A teacher or school coordinator approving club information. |

## 4.3 Start with the Primary User

A project may involve several people, but the team should identify one primary user to guide its initial ideas.

A clearly defined primary user helps the team:

* Ask focused questions.
* Identify relevant evidence.
* Understand the user's experience.
* Avoid designing a vague solution intended for "everyone."

# 5. Perspective: The Same Situation Can Feel Different

People experiencing the same situation may have different goals, information, responsibilities, and barriers.

Perspective-taking helps students understand that their own experience is only one possible way of seeing the problem.

| Situation                | Person      | Possible Goal                                               | Possible Difficulty                                                        |
| ------------------------ | ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| School club registration | New student | Find a suitable club and register correctly.                | Does not know where reliable information is published.                     |
| School club registration | Club leader | Receive complete registrations before the deadline.         | Answers the same questions repeatedly and receives incomplete information. |
| School club registration | Coordinator | Keep the process fair and organised.                        | Information from different clubs may be inconsistent.                      |
| Assignment deadlines     | Student     | Complete work on time without forgetting tasks.             | Receives deadlines from several subjects in different places.              |
| Assignment deadlines     | Teacher     | Help students understand expectations and submit correctly. | Students may overlook updates or ask questions after the deadline.         |

## 5.1 Perspective Is Not the Same as Knowing the Answer

Role-play helps teams explore possible perspectives, generate useful questions, and recognise different viewpoints.

However, a role-play response is **not automatically evidence**.

Statements created during role-play should be treated as hypotheses unless they are supported by:

* A real observation.
* A repeated experience.
* An actual interview response.
* Other reliable evidence.

### Useful Language

* "The user may experience..."
* "We think this could happen because..."
* "We need to find out whether..."

---

# 6. Observation, Assumption, Evidence, and Question

Strong user discovery depends on understanding the difference between:

* What the team directly observed.
* What the team believes.
* What supports or challenges that belief.
* What still needs to be investigated.

| Term            | Meaning                                                   | Example                                                            | How to Use It                                                          |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Observation** | Something directly seen, heard, or experienced.           | Three students asked where the club registration form was located. | Record it clearly without adding an explanation that was not observed. |
| **Assumption**  | An explanation or belief that has not yet been tested.    | Students do not register because they are not interested.          | Mark it as untested and turn it into a question.                       |
| **Evidence**    | Information that supports or challenges an understanding. | Two students said they wanted to register but missed the deadline. | Use it to refine the need and show why it matters.                     |
| **Question**    | Something the team still needs to investigate.            | What information do students need before deciding to join?         | Explore it during an interview or later test.                          |

## 6.1 One Situation, Four Different Statements

| Statement                                                                          | Type                      | Reason                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "Four students checked two different group chats for the same deadline."           | **Observation**           | It describes visible behaviour without explaining the cause. |
| "Students are careless with deadlines."                                            | **Assumption / Judgment** | It assigns a cause and labels the users without evidence.    |
| "Three interviewed students said deadline updates are posted in different places." | **Evidence**              | It records information provided directly by users.           |
| "Where do students currently check when they want to confirm a deadline?"          | **Question**              | It explores current behaviour without suggesting an answer.  |

## 6.2 Turn Assumptions into Questions

| Assumption                                     | Better Question                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Students forget because they are disorganised. | What makes it difficult to remember or track assignments during busy weeks?                     |
| Students want an app.                          | How do students currently find, record, or check the information?                               |
| The problem happens every day.                 | When does the problem happen most often, and when does it not happen?                           |
| Everyone is affected in the same way.          | Which students experience the greatest difficulty, and what is different about their situation? |

# 7. Asking Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions encourage users to describe their experiences in their own words.

They often begin with:

**What, when, where, how, or why.**

Useful questions focus on a real past or current experience rather than asking users to approve the team's idea.

| Question Type           | Weak or Leading Question                        | Stronger Open Question                                                   |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Current Behaviour**   | Would you use an app to remember deadlines?     | How do you currently keep track of assignments and deadlines?            |
| **Specific Experience** | Is school information confusing?                | Tell me about the last time you tried to find school information.        |
| **Difficulty**          | Do you forget because there are too many tasks? | What is the most difficult part of managing several tasks?               |
| **Importance**          | Would a better system help you?                 | What happens when information is late, incomplete, or difficult to find? |
| **Workaround**          | Do you need reminders?                          | What do you do now when you are unsure about a deadline?                 |
| **Improvement**         | Do you like our idea?                           | What would make the current experience easier or clearer for you?        |

## 7.1 A Simple Interview Flow

### 1. Introduce

Explain the topic and the short purpose of the conversation.

### 2. Ask

Ask one clear, open-ended question at a time.

### 3. Listen

Allow the user to finish their answer without interruption.

### 4. Probe

Ask for an example, detail, or explanation.

### 5. Confirm

Paraphrase the main point and check that you understood it correctly.

### 6. Thank

End respectfully without making promises.

## 7.2 Useful Follow-Up Prompts

* "Can you tell me more about that?"
* "What happened the last time?"
* "When does that usually happen?"
* "What do you do next?"
* "Why is that part important to you?"
* "What makes it easier or more difficult?"
* "Did I understand correctly that...?"

# 8. Listening for Needs, Frustrations, Goals, and Current Behaviour

Users may not directly state what they need.

Teams should listen for patterns in:

* What the user is trying to achieve.
* What prevents the user from making progress.
* What the user currently does.
* What effect the situation has on the user.

| Evidence Category       | What It Describes                                     | Example User Statement                                                              | Possible Meaning                                            |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Need**                | What would make the experience work better.           | "I need one reliable place to confirm the final deadline."                          | The user needs consistent and trustworthy information.      |
| **Frustration**         | What creates difficulty, delay, confusion, or stress. | "The deadline changed in one group, but the other group still showed the old date." | Inconsistent information creates uncertainty.               |
| **Goal**                | What the user is trying to achieve.                   | "I want to finish early enough to ask for help if I need it."                       | The user values planning and time to respond.               |
| **Current Behaviour**   | What the user currently does to manage the situation. | "I ask two friends and check the class chat."                                       | The user relies on multiple sources to confirm information. |
| **Unanswered Question** | What the team still needs to learn.                   | "I'm not sure whether everyone checks the same group."                              | The team needs more evidence about communication channels.  |

## 8.1 A Need Is Not the Same as a Requested Feature

| User Statement              | Requested Feature       | Underlying Need                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I want notifications."     | Automatic notifications | Receive important updates on time and avoid missing changes.          |
| "We need a website."        | Website                 | Find complete and trustworthy information in one accessible place.    |
| "There should be a sensor." | Sensor                  | Know when a condition changes without relying on someone to remember. |

### Key Principle

**A feature is one possible solution. A need describes the useful outcome the user is trying to achieve.**

# 9. Recording and Interpreting Evidence

Evidence notes should be short, accurate, and clearly connected to what the user said or did.

Teams should avoid rewriting a response so strongly that its original meaning changes.

| Evidence Note Element  | What to Record                                                        | Example                                                        |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Context**            | Who was speaking and what situation was being discussed.              | Student managing assignments during exam week.                 |
| **Quote or Behaviour** | A short direct statement or clearly observed action.                  | "I write tasks in three places and sometimes update only one." |
| **Meaning**            | What the evidence may suggest, written cautiously.                    | The student may need one consistent method for updating tasks. |
| **Confidence**         | Whether this is one response, a repeated pattern, or still uncertain. | One interview response; needs comparison with other users.     |
| **Next Question**      | What the team should investigate next.                                | Which information is most often missed or outdated?            |

## 9.1 Evidence Strength at This Stage

| Evidence Level             | Description                                                      | How the Team Should Treat It                                             |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Initial Signal**         | One observation, experience, or user response.                   | A useful starting point, but do not generalise it to everyone.           |
| **Repeated Pattern**       | Similar evidence appears across several responses or situations. | Give it greater weight when defining the need.                           |
| **Contradictory Evidence** | Users describe different experiences or priorities.              | Do not hide the difference. Refine the user group or ask more questions. |
| **Unknown**                | The team has no evidence yet.                                    | Write a question instead of inventing an answer.                         |

## 9.2 Evidence Before Ideas

The purpose of Day 2 is not to prove that the team's first idea is correct.

Instead, the goal is to improve the team's understanding so that Day 3 ideas respond to:

**a clearly identified user + an evidence-based need.**

# 10. Respect, Privacy, and Interview Boundaries

The camp uses short educational role-plays and simple user-discovery conversations.

Students should practise respectful behaviour and avoid collecting sensitive personal information. The goal is to understand an everyday experience, not investigate private matters.

| Practice       | What Students Should Do                                                       | What Students Should Avoid                                                                      |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Permission** | Ask whether the person is willing to answer a few short questions.            | Pressuring someone to participate.                                                              |
| **Purpose**    | Explain the general topic and that the team is learning about the experience. | Pretending the team can immediately fix the problem.                                            |
| **Privacy**    | Record only relevant learning notes without unnecessary personal details.     | Collecting passwords, private account information, medical details, or identifying information. |
| **Respect**    | Listen without mocking, interrupting, or judging the answer.                  | Arguing with the user or correcting their experience.                                           |
| **Promises**   | Thank the user and explain that the idea is still being explored.             | Promising that a specific product or change will be delivered.                                  |
| **Accuracy**   | Confirm important points and clearly label assumptions.                       | Changing a quote to make it support the team's preferred idea.                                  |

## 10.1 Interviewer Responsibilities

* Ask one question at a time and allow enough time for the answer.
* Use neutral language and avoid suggesting a preferred response.
* Take brief notes without losing attention to the speaker.
* Paraphrase important points and ask the user to confirm them.
* Separate the user's words from the team's interpretation.
* End the conversation politely and keep the information within the learning activity.

# 11. Writing a User Need Statement

A User Need Statement turns the team's evidence into one clear focus for idea generation.

It identifies:

1. The primary user.
2. The useful outcome or capability the user needs.
3. The evidence-based reason why the need matters.

The statement **should not include the final solution**.

### User Need Statement Template

**[User] needs a way to [need or useful outcome] because [evidence-based reason].**

## 11.1 Building the Statement

| Part                  | Guiding Question                                          | Example                                                                |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **User**              | Who experiences the difficulty most directly?             | Students managing assignments from several subjects.                   |
| **Need**              | What does the user need to be able to do or achieve?      | See and consistently update all important tasks and deadlines.         |
| **Reason / Evidence** | Why does this matter based on what was observed or heard? | They currently check several places and may miss a change or deadline. |

## 11.2 Strong and Weak Examples

| Statement                                                                                                                                                                           | Evaluation                                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Students need an app with notifications."                                                                                                                                          | **Weak:** It introduces one solution before the team has generated and compared different ideas.               |
| "Everyone needs better organisation."                                                                                                                                               | **Weak:** The user and desired outcome are too broad, and there is no supporting evidence.                     |
| "Students managing assignments from several subjects need a way to see and update deadlines consistently because they currently check multiple sources and sometimes miss changes." | **Strong:** It identifies the user, useful outcome, and evidence-based reason while keeping the solution open. |

## 11.3 Additional User Need Statement Examples

| Area                     | User Need Statement                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Environment**          | Students using shared classrooms need a simple way to know when lights are no longer needed because responsibility is unclear when groups leave at different times. |
| **Digital Safety**       | Students receiving unfamiliar links need a way to recognise warning signs before opening or sharing them because several users rely mainly on the sender's name.    |
| **School Opportunities** | New students interested in clubs need a way to find complete and up-to-date registration information because details are shared across several sources.             |

# 12. User Need Card: Content Standard

The main output for Day 2 is a **User Need Card**.

The card captures what the team has learned about the person behind the Day 1 problem and provides Day 3 idea generation with a clear, user-centred direction.

| User Need Card Field    | What the Team Records                                                               | Quality Check                                                |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Primary User**        | The specific group experiencing the problem most directly.                          | Is the user clear and focused rather than "everyone"?        |
| **Situation**           | Where or when the experience occurs.                                                | Can another person understand the context?                   |
| **Current Difficulty**  | What makes the current experience difficult, slow, unclear, unsafe, or frustrating. | Does it describe the experience without blaming the user?    |
| **User Goal**           | What the user is trying to achieve.                                                 | Is the goal written from the user's point of view?           |
| **User Need Statement** | The user, useful outcome, and evidence-based reason.                                | Does it leave room for several possible ideas?               |
| **Evidence**            | At least two observations, quotes, behaviours, or repeated patterns.                | Are evidence and team interpretation clearly separated?      |
| **Unanswered Question** | One important issue that still requires testing.                                    | Will the question help the team develop or test ideas later? |

## 12.1 User Need Card Quality Criteria

| Criterion           | Strong Evidence of Quality                                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Specific**        | The primary user and situation are clearly defined.                             |
| **Evidence-Based**  | The card includes at least two relevant pieces of evidence.                     |
| **Need-Focused**    | The statement describes a useful outcome rather than a product feature.         |
| **Respectful**      | The language describes the experience without blaming or stereotyping the user. |
| **Open to Ideas**   | Several different solutions could respond to the stated need.                   |
| **Ready for Day 3** | The card gives the team a clear direction for generating and comparing ideas.   |

# 14. Common Misconceptions and Corrections

| Misconception                                                            | Correction                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Our team has the problem, so we already know what every user needs."    | Personal experience is useful, but it does not represent every user. Teams still need questions and evidence.                 |
| "The user is everyone who might see the project."                        | The primary user is the person who experiences the problem or will use the future solution most directly.                     |
| "A role-play answer is automatically evidence."                          | Role-play can reveal perspectives and useful questions, but imagined answers remain assumptions unless supported by evidence. |
| "A good interview explains our idea and asks whether the user likes it." | Day 2 interviews explore the current experience before the team proposes a solution.                                          |
| "If a user asks for a feature, that feature is the need."                | A feature is one possible answer; the need is the useful outcome behind it.                                                   |
| "More notes always mean stronger evidence."                              | Evidence must be relevant, accurate, and clearly connected to the user and the problem.                                       |
| "Different user answers mean the interview failed."                      | Different answers may reveal different user segments, contexts, or priorities that the team needs to understand.              |

---

# 15. Key Takeaways

* A useful project is designed for a specific user, not an undefined general public.
* Primary, secondary, and influencing users may experience the same situation differently.
* Observations and evidence should be separated from assumptions and judgments.
* Open-ended questions explore the user's current experience without leading toward a specific answer.
* Teams should listen for needs, frustrations, goals, current behaviour, and unanswered questions.
* A User Need Statement identifies the user, useful outcome, and evidence-based reason without prescribing a solution.
* The User Need Card becomes the foundation for idea generation on Day 3.

---

# 16. Reflection Questions

1. Which assumption did our team change after listening to the user?
2. Which question produced the most useful information?
3. How will the user's need guide our ideas tomorrow?
