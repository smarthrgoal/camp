1. Day Overview
Day 1 introduces entrepreneurship as a practical way of noticing real problems, understanding why they matter and 
turning them into opportunities for useful value. Students begin with familiar situations from school, community, 
environmental and digital life. They practise separating complaints, problems, solutions and project ideas so they do 
not jump to a product before understanding the need.
Essential Question
How can everyday problems become project opportunities?
Day Purpose
Students discover that entrepreneurship begins with noticing a real problem or unmet need, not with creating a product 
first. They practise turning complaints and observations into clear opportunity statements.
2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the day, students will be able to:
1. Explain entrepreneurship using a simple problem-value-test cycle.
2. Distinguish between a problem, a solution and a project idea.
3. Identify familiar problems that affect a specific group of people.
4. Write an initial opportunity statement that names the situation, the affected user and why it matters.

4. Entrepreneurship: From Problems to Useful Value
In this camp, entrepreneurship means identifying a real problem, creating useful value for people and improving a 
solution through testing. The starting point is not a product or a company name. The starting point is a situation that 
could become better for a specific group of people.

4.3 Value Comes After Understanding
Value is the useful change that a future idea could create for the user. On Day 1, students are not required to design 
that idea yet. Their task is to understand the current difficulty clearly enough that later solutions can respond to a real 
need.
Day 1 Focus
Describe the problem clearly first. Possible solutions will be generated after the user and the need are better understood.

5. Complaint, Problem, Solution and Project Idea
These four terms are connected, but they are not interchangeable. Clear problem discovery requires students to 
recognise which part of the entrepreneurial story they are describing.
Term Meaning Guiding Question Example
Complaint A negative reaction or statement about 
an experience.
What is someone unhappy or 
frustrated about? "School club information is confusing."
Problem A specific difficulty that affects a named 
user in a situation.
Who experiences what difficulty, 
where or when?
Students miss club registration because 
information is scattered across different places.
Solution A proposed way to reduce or remove the 
problem.
What could make the situation 
better? Collect the club information in one place.
Project Idea
A defined concept that connects a user, a 
need, a proposed solution and an 
expected benefit.
What are we proposing, for 
whom and why?
A simple club-finder dashboard that gathers club 
details, deadlines and contacts for students.
5.1 One Situation, Four Different Statements
1
Complaint
"We never know what activities are 
available."
2
Problem
Students cannot find complete activity 
information before registration closes.
3
Solution
Put the information in one accessible 
place.
4
Project Idea
Create a simple activity-finder page 
with dates, requirements and contacts.
5.2 Why the Difference Matters
• A complaint may express frustration but does not always explain the situation clearly.
• A problem names the difficulty and the people affected without deciding the answer too early.
• A solution is one possible response; several different solutions may address the same problem.
• A project idea becomes stronger when it connects the solution to a clear user need and benefit.
Classification Check
If the statement already tells the team what to build, it is probably a solution or project idea rather than the problem itself
6. Problems as Gaps Between Current and Better Experiences
A problem can be understood as a gap between what happens now and what a better experience could look like. 
Describing both sides helps students explain why the situation matters without designing the solution yet.
Current Experience Better Experience The Gap to Explore
Students discover deadlines from different 
messages and often miss updates.
Students can find accurate deadlines when they

Important information is difficult to locate and

6.1 A Clear Problem Description
A useful problem description includes enough detail for another person to understand the situation. It should identify 
the affected group, what happens, the relevant context and the effect of the difficulty.
Element Question Example
Affected User Who experiences the difficulty? Students joining a school club for the first time.
Situation What is happening? They cannot find complete registration information.
Context Where or when does it happen? During the week before club registration closes.
Effect Why does it matter? They may miss the deadline or choose without enough 
information.
7. Observation Before Judgement
Observation records what students see, hear or experience. Judgement adds an explanation, opinion or assumption. 
During problem discovery, observations help the team begin with a shared description of the situation.
Observation Judgement or Assumption More Useful Follow-Up
Three students asked where the club timetable 
was posted.
"The school never shares information 
properly."
Where is the timetable currently shared, and who 
can access it?
Two classroom lights were on after the room 
became empty. "Students do not care about energy." When are lights usually left on, and who notices 
first?
A student paused before opening a link from an 
unknown sender. "Students do not understand cybersecurity." What signs does the student check before opening a 
link?
Observation Sentence Starters
"I noticed that..." | "I heard..." | "This happened when..." | "The person had difficulty with..."

9. What Counts as Initial Evidence?
The Opportunity Map records evidence already available at the end of Day 1. This evidence is an initial starting point, 
not final proof. It may include a direct observation, a repeated experience, a visible pattern or information shared by 
more than one student. Day 2 will help teams question assumptions and collect stronger user evidence.
Initial Evidence Example What It Does Not Prove Yet
Direct Observation Several students asked where the same information was 
posted. Why the information is difficult to find for every user.
Repeated Experience Team members have missed similar updates more than 
once.
That all students experience the problem in the same 
way.
Visible Pattern Waste bins contain many reusable items after lunch. The exact reasons people choose to throw the items 
away.
Shared Report Classmates describe receiving unclear task instructions. Which part of the instructions creates the most difficulty.

10. Need or Nice? Choosing a Problem to Explore
Teams will notice more problems than they can investigate. They therefore compare possible opportunities before 
choosing one. The selected problem should matter to a user and should also be understandable and possible to 
explore within the camp.
Selection Question What the Team Looks For Useful Evidence
Is it important? The difficulty affects a meaningful goal, decision, safety, 
access or everyday experience.
The user loses time, misses an opportunity, experiences 
confusion or faces a preventable risk.
Does it happen often enough? The issue is repeated or likely to appear again, not only a 
one-time accident. Several examples, recurring situations or similar reports.
Does it have a noticeable 
effect?
The problem changes what the user can do, understand or 
achieve.
A missed deadline, wasted resource, unsafe action or 
reduced participation.
Can we understand it? The team can describe the setting and identify people who 
experience it.
A familiar context and accessible users for Day 2 
discovery.
Can we explore it during the 
camp?
The problem is focused enough to investigate, prototype 
and explain in five days.
A clear scope that does not require unavailable data, 
authority or resources.

Selection Principle
Choose a problem that is important enough to matter and focused enough to understand, test and explain.
10.2 Keep the Problem Open
• Do not reject a problem only because the team does not know the solution yet.
• Do not select a problem only because one student already has a favourite product idea.
• Do not make the problem so broad that the affected users and context become unclear.
• Record questions that need investigation instead of filling gaps with assumptions.

11. Writing an Initial Opportunity Statement
An opportunity statement describes the problem clearly enough to guide user discovery. It names the affected user, 
the current difficulty, the relevant situation and why the issue matters. It does not prescribe the final solution.
Opportunity Statement Template
[User group] experiences [specific problem] when or where [situation], which matters because [effect or reason].
11.1 Building the Statement
Part Question Example
User Group Who experiences the problem? Students who are trying to join a school club.
Specific Problem What difficulty do they face? They cannot find complete and consistent registration information.
Situation When or where does it happen? During the days before club registration closes.
Why It Matters What effect does the difficulty create? They may miss the deadline or make a decision without enough 
information.
11.2 Strong and Weak Examples
Statement Why It Is Weak or Strong
"We need an app for school clubs." Weak: it begins with a solution and does not identify the 
exact user problem.
"School information is bad." Weak: the statement is broad, judgemental and does not 
name the user, situation or effect.
"Students trying to join a school club cannot find complete registration information before 
the deadline, so they may miss opportunities or choose without enough detail."
Strong: it identifies the user, difficulty, context and effect 
while leaving the solution open.

12. Opportunity Map: Content Standard
The daily output is an Opportunity Map. It captures the team's current understanding of one selected problem and 
prepares the team for Day 2 user discovery. The map should separate evidence from assumptions and make 
unanswered questions visible.
Opportunity Map Field What the Team Records Quality Check
Selected Problem A focused description of the current difficulty. Does it describe the problem without prescribing 
the final solution?
Affected User Group The specific people who experience the problem most directly. Is the group more specific than "everyone" or "the 
public"?
Where or When The situation, place or time in which the problem occurs. Can another person picture the context?
Evidence Already Available Observations, repeated experiences, patterns or reports 
already known.
Is the team clear about what was actually 
observed and what is still assumed?
Why It Matters The effect on the user's goal, decision, safety, access, time or 
experience.
Does the effect explain why the issue is worth

user on Day 2?

14. Common Misconceptions and Corrections
Misconception Correction
"Entrepreneurship means starting a company immediately." In this camp, entrepreneurship begins with understanding a problem, creating 
value and learning through testing.
"A complaint is already a clear problem." A complaint must be translated into a specific difficulty affecting a named user 
in a context.
"The first solution is usually the best one." Teams keep the problem open so they can understand the user and generate 
several ideas later.
"Our team experiences the problem, so we already know everything." Personal experience is useful initial evidence, but Day 2 will test assumptions 
with user questions and listening.
"Adding technology automatically makes the idea entrepreneurial." Technology is useful only when it supports a real user need and creates clear 
value.
"The biggest problem is always the best camp project." A suitable challenge must be meaningful, focused and possible to explore in 
five days.
15. Key Takeaways
• Entrepreneurship begins with noticing a real problem and creating useful value for people.
• A complaint, problem, solution and project idea describe different parts of the journey.
• Observations are stronger starting points than untested judgements or assumptions.
• A useful opportunity names a specific user, situation, difficulty and meaningful effect.
• Teams should compare problems before choosing one and should leave the solution open on Day 1.
• The Opportunity Map records what the team knows, why the problem matters and what still needs investigation.
16. Reflection Questions
1. What changed when we described the problem without suggesting a solution?
2. Which problem seems most important to the user, not only to our team?
3. What do we still need to learn before creating an idea?
