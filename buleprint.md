2. Position in the Learning Journey
Level 3 is the bridge between the teamwork and problem-solving experience of Level 2 and the more advanced 
startup journey planned for merged Levels 4 and 5. It introduces entrepreneurship directly, but keeps the 
experience practical, playful and age-appropriate.

2.1 Scope Boundaries
Included in Level 3
Problem and opportunity discovery, user needs, observation and questioning, idea generation, simple value 
proposition, low-fidelity prototyping, testing, improvement, teamwork, pitching and reflection.
Reserved for Levels 4 and 5
Full Business Model Canvas, revenue and pricing, cost structures, market size, channels, advanced 
customer validation, startup finance, formal negotiation and a technically functional prototype. Level 3 
prepares students for these ideas without teaching them in full.
3. Level Storyline
Throughout the five days, students work as Young Innovator Teams. Each team chooses a familiar problem from 
school, community, environment or digital life and turns it into a simple project idea.
DECI Summer Camp | Level 3 Blueprint | Page 3
The journey begins with noticing problems rather than jumping to products. Teams then identify who experiences 
the problem, collect evidence, create several possible solutions, explain the value of one selected idea, build a 
low-fidelity prototype and test it with other students.
On Day 5, each team presents the problem, the user, the proposed solution, the prototype, the feedback received 
and the improvement made. The goal is not to launch a real business; it is to understand the entrepreneurial way 
of thinking through an experience students can complete and explain.
4. Overall Learning Objectives
1. Explain entrepreneurship as identifying a real problem, creating useful value and improving a solution through testing.
2. Identify a specific user group and describe a need using observations, questions and evidence rather than 
assumptions alone.
3. Generate several project ideas and select one using simple criteria such as usefulness, feasibility and fit with 
available skills.
4. Write a simple value proposition that connects the user, the need, the proposed solution and the expected benefit.
5. Build and test a low-fidelity prototype, then revise at least one part based on relevant feedback.
6. Deliver a clear team pitch that communicates the problem, value, prototype, learning and next step.
5. Final Challenge
The Young Innovators Challenge
Each team identifies a familiar problem, understands who is affected, develops a useful project idea, creates 
a simple prototype, tests it and presents the improved concept during a final Demo Day.
Suggested Challenge Areas
• Helping students organise assignments, deadlines or shared school tasks.
• Reducing waste, saving energy or encouraging responsible environmental habits.
• Making school information, clubs or opportunities easier to find.
• Supporting safer and more respectful digital behaviour.
• Improving inclusion, communication or participation among students.
• Helping students manage stress, busy periods or everyday study routines.
• Improving a simple community service, public space or neighbourhood experience.
• Using a DECI technical interest to support a practical user need.
Technical Skill Connection Menu
Teams may connect their idea to a DECI technical interest without needing to build a complete technical product:
• Web Development: a paper website, dashboard or app-screen flow.
• AI: a simple decision flow, recommendation feature or responsible AI use case.
• Embedded Systems: a device sketch, sensor idea or input-output flow.
• Digital Art: a visual campaign, interface concept or project identity.
• Cybersecurity: a safer user journey, protection rule or awareness tool.

DAY
1
Spot the Opportunity
Entrepreneurship Mindset and Problem Discovery
How can everyday problems become project opportunities?
Day Purpose
Students discover that entrepreneurship begins with noticing a real problem or unmet need, not with creating a 
product first. They practise turning complaints and observations into clear opportunity statements.
Learning Objectives
1. Explain entrepreneurship using a simple problem-value-test cycle.
2. Distinguish between a problem, a solution and a project idea.
3. Identify familiar problems that affect a specific group of people.
4. Write an initial opportunity statement that names the situation, the affected user and why it matters.
Key Content
• Entrepreneurship as solving a real problem by creating useful value.
• The difference between a complaint, a problem, a solution and a project idea.
• Problems as gaps between the current experience and a better experience.
• Observation before judgement: what students see, hear or experience.
• Opportunity areas in school, community, environment and digital life.
• A simple opportunity statement: Who experiences what problem, where or when, and why does it matter?
• Choosing a problem that is important, understandable and possible to explore during the camp.
Suggested Learning Sequence
Session Stage Activity / Content Purpose or Focus
Icebreaker What Would You Fix? Students draw a familiar place card and race to list small problems they 
notice there.
Activity 1 Problem, Solution or Idea? Teams sort scenario cards and explain the difference between the 
problem, a proposed solution and a complete project idea.
Content Reflection What Entrepreneurs Actually Do The instructor introduces the cycle: notice a problem, understand 
people, create value, test, improve and share.
Break 1 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 2 Opportunity Hunt Teams explore challenge cards from school, community, environment 
and digital life and record observable problems.
Activity 3 Need or Nice? Students compare problems by importance, frequency and effect, 
without choosing a solution yet.
Break 2 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 4 Build the Opportunity Map Each team selects one problem and records who is affected, what 
happens, why it matters and what the team still needs to learn.
Daily Output
Spot the Opportunity Output
DECI Summer Camp | Level 3 Blueprint | Page 6
An Opportunity Map containing the selected problem, affected user group, evidence already available, why 
the issue matters and questions for further investigation.
Connection to the Final Challenge
The Opportunity Map becomes the starting point for Day 2 user discovery. Teams must understand the people 
behind the problem before designing any solution.
Reflection Questions
• What changed when we described the problem without suggesting a solution?
• Which problem seems most important to the user, not only to our team?
• What do we still need to learn before creating an idea?

DAY
2
Understand the People
User Empathy and Needs
How can we understand what people really need?
Day Purpose
Students learn to design for real people rather than for their own assumptions. Through role-play and evidence 
sorting, they identify users, needs, frustrations, goals and questions that can guide a useful solution.
Learning Objectives
1. Identify the primary user affected by the selected problem.
2. Distinguish between an observation, an assumption and a question.
3. Use open questions to explore a user experience in a short role-play interview.
4. Write a user need statement supported by at least two pieces of evidence.
Key Content
• The difference between a user and the general public.
• Primary users, secondary users and people who influence the experience.
• Observation, assumption and evidence.
• Open questions that begin with what, when, where, how and why.
• Listening for needs, frustrations, goals and current behaviour.
• A simple user need statement: [User] needs a way to [need] because [evidence or reason].
• Respect, privacy and avoiding promises during interviews.
Suggested Learning Sequence
Session Stage Activity / Content Purpose or Focus
Icebreaker Whose Problem Is It? Teams match short problem cards to the user most directly affected 
and explain their choice.
Activity 1 Walk in Their Shoes Students use user-role cards to experience the same situation from 
different perspectives.
Content Reflection Ask Before You Assume The instructor models the difference between observation, assumption, 
evidence and a useful open question.
Break 1 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 2 Mini User Interview Students role-play user interviews in pairs and collect short evidence 
notes using open questions.
Activity 3 Needs Detective Teams sort user quotes into needs, frustrations, goals, current 
behaviour and unanswered questions.
Break 2 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 4 Create the User Need Card Teams define the primary user, write the need statement and record the 
evidence that supports it.
Daily Output
Understand the People Output
DECI Summer Camp | Level 3 Blueprint | Page 8
A User Need Card containing the primary user, situation, need, current difficulty, evidence, user goal and one 
question that still requires testing.
Connection to the Final Challenge
The User Need Card prevents teams from generating random ideas on Day 3. Every proposed idea must respond 
to the named user and need.
Reflection Questions
• Which assumption did our team change after listening to the user?
• Which question produced the most useful information?
• How will the user need guide our ideas tomorrow?

DAY
3
Create the Value
Idea Generation and Simple Value Proposition
How can we create an idea that is useful and worth choosing?
Day Purpose
Students generate several possible solutions, use creative prompts and technical-interest lenses, then select one 
idea that creates clear value for the user and can be represented through a simple prototype.
Learning Objectives
1. Generate several different solutions for one defined user need.
2. Improve or combine ideas using creative prompts and technical skill lenses.
3. Compare ideas using usefulness, feasibility and fit with available skills.
4. Write a simple value proposition connecting the user, need, solution and benefit.
Key Content
• Divergent thinking before selection: quantity and variety first.
• Idea prompts: simplify, combine, remove, automate, visualise, connect and personalise.
• Using DECI technical interests as a lens rather than forcing technology into every solution.
• The difference between a feature and a user benefit.
• Simple selection criteria: useful, understandable, feasible, testable and connected to the need.
• A simple value proposition: For [user] who [need], our idea [solution] helps them [benefit].
• Choosing one idea while keeping useful elements from other options.
Suggested Learning Sequence
Session Stage Activity / Content Purpose or Focus
Icebreaker Impossible Ideas Teams create deliberately unusual solutions, then rescue one useful 
element from each idea.
Activity 1 Idea Relay Students build on one another ideas in timed rounds without criticism or 
debate.
Content Reflection From Feature to Value The instructor shows how a solution feature creates a specific benefit 
for a named user.
Break 1 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 2 Technical Lens Cards Teams apply web, AI, embedded systems, digital art or cybersecurity 
lenses to improve selected ideas.
Activity 3 Would It Help? Teams compare their strongest ideas using usefulness, feasibility and fit 
with the user need.
Break 2 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 4 Create the Project Idea Card Teams define the selected idea, key benefit, technical connection, 
simple value proposition and main assumption to test.
Daily Output
Create the Value Output
DECI Summer Camp | Level 3 Blueprint | Page 10
A Project Idea Card and Simple Value Proposition containing the user, need, selected solution, benefit, 
technical connection, reason for selection and main assumption to test.
Connection to the Final Challenge
The selected idea and its main assumption determine what the team must represent and test through the Day 4 
prototype.
Reflection Questions
• Which idea-generation rule helped us create more variety?
• What is the difference between a feature and a benefit?
• What is the most important assumption our prototype should test?

DAY
4
Build, Test and Improve
Prototyping and Feedback
How can a simple prototype help us learn quickly?
Day Purpose
Students learn that a prototype is a quick model used to answer questions, not a finished product. They build a 
low-fidelity version, test it with peers and improve one or more parts using specific feedback.
Learning Objectives
1. Select a suitable low-fidelity prototype format for the project idea.
2. Build a prototype that makes the main user experience visible or testable.
3. Collect specific feedback using agreed testing questions.
4. Revise at least one part of the prototype and explain the reason for the change.
Key Content
• Prototype as a tool for learning before investing time and resources.
• Low-fidelity formats: paper screen, storyboard, device sketch, service role-play, process map or campaign mock-up.
• Testing one main question rather than asking whether people simply like the idea.
• Useful testing questions: What is clear? What is confusing? What would you do next? What is missing?
• Feedback as evidence, not an order; teams decide what to use and why.
• Iteration: build, test, learn and improve.
• Recording changes so the team can explain its learning during Demo Day.
Suggested Learning Sequence
Session Stage Activity / Content Purpose or Focus
Icebreaker Prototype Match-Up Teams match project ideas to the prototype format that would test them 
most quickly.
Activity 1 Build It in Five Students complete a fast paper-building challenge and reflect on what a 
rough model can reveal.
Content Reflection Prototype to Learn, Not to Impress The instructor introduces low-fidelity prototyping, testing questions and 
iteration.
Break 1 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 2 Prototype Sprint Teams build the first version of their selected idea using paper and 
simple materials.
Activity 3 Test with Three Questions Another team interacts with the prototype while the creators observe 
and record evidence without defending the idea.
Break 2 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 4 Improve and Document Teams select relevant feedback, revise the prototype and record what 
changed, why it changed and what remains uncertain.
Daily Output
Build, Test and Improve Output
DECI Summer Camp | Level 3 Blueprint | Page 12
A Low-Fidelity Prototype and a Test and Improvement Log recording the testing question, observations, 
feedback, changes made and remaining uncertainty.
Connection to the Final Challenge
The prototype and improvement evidence become the centre of the Day 5 Demo Day story. Teams must show not 
only what they made, but also what they learned.
Reflection Questions
• What did the prototype reveal that discussion alone did not?
• Which feedback was evidence-based and useful?
• What did we change, and why?

DAY
5
Pitch the Possibility
Storytelling and Demo Day
How can we clearly show the problem, value and learning?
Day Purpose
Students organise the full entrepreneurial story, explain their project clearly, demonstrate the prototype, respond 
to questions and reflect on how the idea improved across the five-day journey.
Learning Objectives
1. Organise a short pitch into problem, user, evidence, idea, value, prototype, learning and next step.
2. Share presentation responsibilities and use clear transitions between speakers.
3. Demonstrate the prototype and explain at least one change made after testing.
4. Respond to audience questions using evidence and acknowledge what remains uncertain.
Key Content
• The purpose of a pitch: helping an audience understand the problem, value and next step.
• A simple pitch structure: problem, user, evidence, idea, value, prototype, testing, improvement and ask or next step.
• Using a prototype as evidence rather than decoration.
• Sharing roles and creating smooth transitions.
• Clear voice, suitable pace, eye contact, posture and concise notes.
• Answering questions honestly: know, infer, do not yet know, and need to test.
• Reflecting on teamwork, learning and how the idea could develop in a future level.
Suggested Learning Sequence
Session Stage Activity / Content Purpose or Focus
Icebreaker Pitch Puzzle Teams arrange shuffled pitch sections into a logical entrepreneurial 
story.
Activity 1 Build the Demo Story Teams complete the Mini Project One-Pager and assign speaking and 
demonstration roles.
Content Reflection Make the Value Visible The instructor models a short pitch that connects the problem, user 
evidence, prototype and learning.
Break 1 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 2 Feedback Carousel Teams deliver a practice pitch to another team and receive feedback on 
clarity, value, evidence and prototype use.
Activity 3 Revise and Rehearse Teams select useful feedback, improve the pitch and rehearse 
transitions, timing and responses to questions.
Break 2 15-minute break Students take a short break.
Activity 4 Young Innovators Demo Day Each team delivers a 3-4-minute pitch, demonstrates the prototype and 
responds to brief audience questions.
Daily Output
Pitch the Possibility Output

A Mini Project One-Pager, an improved low-fidelity prototype and a 3-4-minute Demo Day pitch that explains 
the problem, user, value, testing evidence, improvement and next step.
Connection to the Final Challenge
The Demo Day completes the Level 3 introduction to entrepreneurship and prepares students for the deeper 
startup, Business Model Canvas and mixed-technical-team experience of Levels 4 and 5.
Reflection Questions
• Which part of our idea changed most during the week?
• What evidence made our pitch stronger?
• What would we need to learn or build next before this idea became a real project?
• Which role did each team member contribute to the final outcome?

7. Standard Daily Session Structure
Each day follows the approved Level 2 delivery rhythm so trainers and students experience a consistent session 
structure. The total time is 180 minutes, including two 15-minute breaks and 150 minutes of learning time.
8. Assessment Strategy
Assessment is formative and embedded in games, team discussions, practical outputs, prototype testing and 
reflection. There is no formal exam.
Formative Assessment
• Ability to define a problem before proposing a solution.
• Use of observations, questions and evidence to understand the user.
• Quantity and variety of ideas generated before selection.
• Connection between the user need, solution and expected value.
• Quality and testability of the low-fidelity prototype.
• Use of specific feedback to make an explained improvement.
• Balanced contribution, role ownership and respectful teamwork.
• Clarity of the final pitch and honesty when answering questions.
Evidence of Learning
• Opportunity Map.
• User Need Card and interview notes.
• Project Idea Card and Simple Value Proposition.
• Low-Fidelity Prototype.
• Prototype Test and Improvement Log.
• Mini Project One-Pager.
• Peer Feedback Notes and Demo Day Pitch.

Final Success Indicators
• The team identifies a specific problem and a clearly named user group.
• The idea responds to evidence rather than assumptions alone.
• The value proposition connects the user, need, solution and benefit.
• The prototype makes the main experience visible or testable.
• The team explains at least one improvement made after feedback.
• Every team member contributes to the final Demo Day experience.
9. Required Materials
• A4 paper, coloured paper or index cards.
• Pens, pencils and coloured markers.
• Sticky notes or small paper slips.
• Scissors, masking tape and glue sticks where available.
• Recycled cardboard, paper cups, string and simple craft materials for prototyping.
• Printed challenge, user, creative prompt, technical lens and feedback cards.
• Simple worksheets for the five daily outputs.
• Board or presentation screen and a visible timer.
Low-Resource Requirement
Every activity must have a paper-and-pen alternative. No activity requires learner devices, internet access, 
programming, hardware components or a technically functional product.
10. Instructor Role
The instructor acts as a facilitator, user-research coach, challenge sponsor and Demo Day panel member by:
• Keeping the experience playful while connecting each game to an entrepreneurial concept.
• Stopping teams from jumping to solutions before defining the problem and user.
• Using familiar examples and avoiding advanced business terminology.
• Modelling open questions, evidence-based feedback and honest responses to uncertainty.
• Supporting balanced roles without selecting the idea or completing the output for the team.
• Encouraging use of DECI technical interests only when they create genuine value for the user.
• Treating a rough prototype as a learning tool, not judging visual polish or technical complexity.
• Protecting respectful testing and ensuring feedback focuses on the work rather than the student.
• Connecting every daily output to the final Demo Day story.
• Highlighting that Levels 4 and 5 will add Business Model Canvas, startup structure and deeper prototype 
development.
