# Pitch Puzzle

## Pitch Puzzle Card Set

**GROUP ACTIVITY**

### PROBLEM / OPPORTUNITY

Describe the situation and explain why it matters.

### PRIMARY USER

Identify who experiences the problem and describe the situation in which it occurs.

### USER EVIDENCE

Share one observation, quote, behaviour, or fact gathered during the camp.

### SELECTED PROJECT IDEA

Explain the proposed solution in simple, accessible language.

### USER VALUE

Explain what becomes easier, safer, clearer, faster, more accessible, or more useful for the user.

### PROTOTYPE DEMONSTRATION

Show one important user task or interaction.

### TESTING EVIDENCE

Describe what happened when another person used or explored the prototype.

### IMPROVEMENT

Explain what was changed and why the change was made.

### NEXT STEP / REMAINING QUESTION

State what still needs to be tested, learned, improved, or built.

### DISTRACTION: EVERY FEATURE

Do not list every current and future feature in detail.

### DISTRACTION: UNSUPPORTED CLAIM

Do not claim that the idea is perfect, guaranteed to work, or useful for everyone.

### DISTRACTION: LONG TECHNICAL DETAIL

Do not explain technical terms or components that do not support the user value.

### DISTRACTION: LONG TEAM INTRODUCTION

Do not spend the opening introducing every team member or thanking the audience at length.

# Level 3 – Day 5 Content Document

## Pitch the Possibility

### Storytelling and Demo Day

**Programme:** DECI Summer Camp
**Level:** Level 3 Students
**Day Duration:** 3 hours, including two 15-minute breaks
**Delivery Language:** English
**Learning Approach:** Game-based, presentation-driven, and reflective

**Daily Output:** Mini Project One-Pager + Improved Prototype + 3–4-Minute Demo Day Pitch

---

## 1. Day Overview

Day 5 brings the Level 3 Young Innovators journey together. Teams organise their problem, user evidence, project idea, value proposition, prototype, and testing results into one clear entrepreneurial story.

Students create a Mini Project One-Pager, practise their pitch with another team, revise their message, and deliver a 3–4-minute Demo Day presentation that includes a short prototype demonstration and brief audience questions.

### Essential Question

**How can we clearly show the problem, value, and learning?**

### Day Purpose

Students learn that a strong Demo Day presentation does more than present an idea. It explains:

* why the problem matters;
* who needs the solution;
* what the team tested;
* what the team learned;
* what changed as a result; and
* what the team would explore next.

---

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the day, students will be able to:

1. Organise the five-day project journey into a clear problem–user–value–prototype–learning story.
2. Deliver a coordinated 3–4-minute team pitch supported by a purposeful prototype demonstration.
3. Give and use specific peer feedback to improve clarity, evidence, and delivery.
4. Respond to brief audience questions using project evidence and honest next-step thinking.

## 2.1 Success Criteria

By the end of the day:

* The audience can identify the problem, primary user, and main value.
* The prototype demonstration makes one core user experience visible.
* The team explains one testing insight and one evidence-based improvement.
* Every team member contributes.
* The complete pitch remains within 3–4 minutes.
* The closing identifies an honest remaining uncertainty or next step.

# 4. From Project Work to a Demo Story

The worksheets from Days 1–4 contain more information than can fit into a short presentation. Teams must select the strongest evidence and connect each stage into one clear story.

The audience should understand how the team moved from an everyday problem to an improved project idea.

### The Five-Part Story

**1. PROBLEM**
What is happening, and why does it matter?

**2. USER**
Who experiences the need, and what did we learn?

**3. VALUE**
What useful change does the idea offer?

**4. PROTOTYPE**
How did we make the idea visible and testable?

**5. LEARNING**
What evidence changed or strengthened the idea?

## 4.1 The Five-Day Evidence Trail

| Day   | Project Evidence                        | How It Appears in the Pitch                                                        |
| ----- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Day 1 | Opportunity Map                         | The problem, affected group, and reason the opportunity matters.                   |
| Day 2 | User Need Card                          | The primary user, situation, need, and user evidence.                              |
| Day 3 | Project Idea Card + Value Proposition   | The selected solution, intended benefit, and main assumption.                      |
| Day 4 | Prototype + Test and Improvement Log    | The tested experience, evidence collected, change made, and remaining uncertainty. |
| Day 5 | Mini Project One-Pager + Demo Day Pitch | One concise story showing the journey, prototype, learning, and next step.         |

### Selection Principle

Do not read every worksheet during the pitch.

Use each worksheet as evidence, then select only the details the audience needs to understand the story.

---

# 5. What Demo Day Is — and Is Not

| Demo Day Is                                                        | Demo Day Is Not                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A chance to explain a problem, user need, and useful project idea. | A competition to prove that every idea is ready to become a company.              |
| A short demonstration of a low-fidelity prototype.                 | A requirement to build a complete app, device, AI model, or cybersecurity system. |
| Evidence that the team tested and improved the idea.               | A performance that hides confusion, failed tests, or remaining questions.         |
| A coordinated team story with shared participation.                | One confident speaker presenting while the rest of the team remains invisible.    |
| A reflection on how DECI skills can contribute to solutions.       | A technical lecture that lists tools without explaining user value.               |
| An opportunity to receive questions and identify next steps.       | A promise of success, revenue, impact, or technical performance without evidence. |

## 5.1 The Right Level of Ambition

Level 3 teams present a **tested project possibility**.

They are not expected to present:

* a complete Business Model Canvas;
* a revenue model;
* detailed market sizing;
* legal structures; or
* production-ready technology.

These deeper startup decisions belong to Levels 4 and 5.

A successful Level 3 pitch demonstrates **clear thinking, evidence, iteration, and learning**.

### Honest Positioning

**Say:**
“We created and tested an early prototype.”

**Do not say:**
“Our final product is proven to work.”

Use stronger claims only when the evidence genuinely supports them.

---

# 6. Audience and Purpose

A pitch becomes clearer when the team understands what the audience needs to know.

The audience has not completed the project worksheets, so presenters must provide enough context to make the idea understandable without explaining every discussion or activity.

| Audience Question                     | Pitch Section                         |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| What problem are you trying to solve? | Problem and opportunity               |
| Who experiences the problem?          | Primary user and user evidence        |
| What are you proposing?               | Project idea and solution explanation |
| Why would the user care?              | Benefit and value proposition         |
| How can I understand the experience?  | Prototype demonstration               |
| Did anyone respond to the idea?       | Testing evidence and feedback         |
| What changed after testing?           | Version 1 → Version 2 improvement     |
| What still needs to be learned?       | Remaining uncertainty and next step   |

## 6.1 One Pitch, One Main Message

Before creating the full pitch, the team should be able to explain the project in one sentence.

### One-Sentence Project Template

**We are helping [primary user] who experiences [specific problem] by creating [project idea] so that [main user benefit].**

This sentence keeps the story focused and helps presenters avoid unrelated features or technical details.

# 7. The Young Innovators Pitch Structure

| Section                    | Question Answered                      | Recommended Content                                                             |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Opening                 | Why should the audience pay attention? | A short relatable situation, question, or observation connected to the problem. |
| 2. Problem                 | What is happening?                     | A clear problem statement and who is affected.                                  |
| 3. User Evidence           | How do we know the need matters?       | One or two useful observations, quotes, behaviours, or findings.                |
| 4. Project Idea            | What are we proposing?                 | A simple explanation of the idea and user experience.                           |
| 5. Value                   | Why is the idea useful?                | The main benefit and value proposition.                                         |
| 6. Prototype Demo          | What does the experience look like?    | One core flow, interaction, process, or response.                               |
| 7. Testing and Improvement | What did we learn?                     | Testing question, important evidence, and one meaningful change.                |
| 8. Next Step               | What would we explore next?            | One realistic next test, improvement, or technical question.                    |
| 9. Closing                 | What should the audience remember?     | Restate the problem, user value, and project possibility in one line.           |

### 7.1 Logical Connections

* User evidence should explain why the selected solution is relevant.
* The value proposition should describe a benefit, not simply list features.
* The prototype demonstration should show the promised value in action.
* Testing evidence should connect to the main assumption from Day 3.
* Improvements should respond to evidence rather than personal preference.
* The next step should address a remaining uncertainty rather than introduce an unrelated project.

---

# 8. Making the Value Visible

A strong pitch makes the connection between the user problem, solution, and expected benefit easy to understand.

Simply naming features is not enough.

The team should explain what becomes:

* easier;
* safer;
* clearer;
* faster;
* more accessible; or
* more useful.

| Feature-Led Statement                      | Value-Led Statement                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Our app has reminder notifications.”      | “Students receive a clear reminder before a deadline, helping them avoid missed tasks.”                       |
| “Our device uses a classroom sensor.”      | “The classroom receives a simple alert when energy is being wasted, helping users take action quickly.”       |
| “Our idea includes AI recommendations.”    | “The user receives suggestions based on selected needs, with a clear explanation of why each option appears.” |
| “Our website has a secure login.”          | “Users can access personalised information while sharing only the data needed for the service.”               |
| “Our campaign uses digital illustrations.” | “The message becomes easier to notice and understand for the intended audience.”                              |

## 8.1 Value Statement Check

The value statement should:

* name the primary user;
* connect directly to the selected need;
* explain the useful change or benefit;
* avoid unsupported promises such as “perfect,” “guaranteed,” or “for everyone”; and
* be visible or testable through the prototype and evidence.

### Value Test

After hearing the value statement, the audience should be able to answer:

**“Why would this user choose or use the idea?”**

---

# 9. Evidence Before Claims

Teams should use evidence gathered during the camp instead of making broad claims.

Evidence does not need to be large or scientific at this level. It needs to be accurate and connected to what the team actually learned.

| Claim Type         | Weak Version                              | Evidence-Based Version                                                                                            |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Problem Importance | “All students have this problem.”         | “In our activity, several students described missing club registration information and deadlines.”                |
| User Need          | “Users need an app.”                      | “The users we explored needed a clear way to compare options and know the next action.”                           |
| Prototype Clarity  | “The prototype is easy.”                  | “Our tester completed the comparison step but paused before registration, so we clarified the next-action label.” |
| Improvement        | “Version 2 is better.”                    | “We moved the safety message before confirmation because the tester missed it at the end of the flow.”            |
| Future Impact      | “This will solve the problem completely.” | “The idea may reduce confusion, but it still needs testing with more users and realistic information.”            |

## 9.1 Useful Evidence Sources

* **Observation:** A tester selected the wrong action or hesitated at a label.
* **Direct Quote:** “I thought this icon meant settings.”
* **Task Result:** The tester completed the first two steps but could not find the final action.
* **Comparison:** Version 2 reduced the number of unclear choices.
* **Team Decision:** The team removed a feature because it did not support the user need or test question.
* **Remaining Question:** The team still needs to learn whether real users trust the suggested information source.

# 10. Mini Project One-Pager: Content Standard

The Mini Project One-Pager is the team’s concise written summary and planning tool for Demo Day.

It should fit the full story onto one page using short statements, selected evidence, and a simple prototype reference.

It is **not a full report**.

| One-Pager Field      | What the Team Includes                                            | Quality Check                                             |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Project Name         | A short, clear name.                                              | Does the name fit the idea and audience?                  |
| Problem              | One focused statement describing the situation and affected user. | Is it specific rather than a general complaint?           |
| Primary User         | The main person or group the team designed for.                   | Is the user consistent with the evidence and prototype?   |
| Evidence             | One or two observations, quotes, or findings.                     | Does it support the need without exaggeration?            |
| Project Idea         | A simple explanation of the proposed experience or solution.      | Can another person understand it in one or two sentences? |
| Value Proposition    | User, need, idea, and intended benefit.                           | Does it explain value rather than list features?          |
| Prototype            | Format and core user experience being demonstrated.               | Does the demo show the promised value?                    |
| Testing and Learning | Main test question, strongest evidence, and important finding.    | Is the learning connected to the main assumption?         |
| Improvement          | Meaningful change made after testing and why.                     | Can the audience identify Version 1 and Version 2?        |
| Next Step            | One realistic test, improvement, or technical question.           | Does it address remaining uncertainty?                    |
| DECI Skills Used     | Technical or creative skills that supported the idea.             | Are the skills connected to a visible contribution?       |

## 10.1 One-Pager Writing Style

* Use short sentences, labels, and evidence points instead of long paragraphs.
* Use consistent project terminology throughout the page and pitch.
* Clearly separate observations from assumptions.
* Clearly label simulated or unbuilt technical behaviour.
* Keep only information that supports the Demo Day story.

# 11. Sharing Team Roles

A Demo Day pitch should demonstrate teamwork.

Roles should be assigned according to the story, prototype requirements, and each member’s readiness.

Every student should understand the full project, even when presenting only one section.

| Role                         | Main Responsibility                                                 | Important Note                                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Opening and Problem Speaker  | Introduce the situation, problem, and affected user.                | Keep the opening brief and connected to evidence.                       |
| User and Value Speaker       | Explain the user insight, idea, and value proposition.              | Make the benefit understandable without unnecessary technical language. |
| Prototype Demonstrator       | Guide the audience through one core user experience.                | Coordinate with the speaker and avoid unnecessary features.             |
| Testing and Learning Speaker | Explain the test, evidence, improvement, and remaining uncertainty. | Explain what changed and why.                                           |
| Closing and Questions Lead   | State the next step, close the story, and direct questions.         | Let other members answer questions connected to their evidence.         |
| Timekeeper / Materials Lead  | Prepare the prototype, one-pager, and timing signals.               | This role may be combined with a speaking role.                         |

## 11.1 Team Participation Standard

* Every student has a visible speaking, demonstration, or question-response role.
* Transitions are planned so the pitch feels like one team story.
* All team members can explain the problem, user, value, and main learning.
* The prototype operator knows exactly when and how the demonstration begins and ends.
* The team agrees who will respond first to different types of questions.

---

# 12. Managing the 3–4-Minute Pitch

A short pitch requires careful selection.

Teams should not speak faster simply to include every worksheet. Instead, they should protect the most important story and remove repetition, long introductions, and unnecessary technical detail.

| Pitch Part            | Suggested Time | Focus                                              |
| --------------------- | -------------: | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Opening + Problem     |      25–35 sec | Create relevance and state the focused problem.    |
| User + Evidence       |      30–40 sec | Identify the primary user and key insights.        |
| Idea + Value          |      35–45 sec | Explain the project and why it is useful.          |
| Prototype Demo        |      50–70 sec | Show one core journey or interaction.              |
| Testing + Improvement |      40–55 sec | Explain the test, evidence, and change.            |
| Next Step + Closing   |      20–30 sec | State remaining uncertainty and the final message. |

## 12.1 Timing Decisions

### If the Pitch Is Too Long

* Remove repeated background information.
* Shorten the feature list to the core experience.
* Demonstrate one path instead of every possible path.
* Replace long technical explanations with user value.
* Move optional details to the Q&A.

### If the Pitch Is Too Short

* Add one useful evidence point.
* Clarify how the user experiences the idea.
* Show the Version 1 → Version 2 change.
* State one realistic next step.
* Strengthen transitions between sections.

### Timing Rule

**A clear three-minute story is stronger than a rushed four-minute list of everything the team produced.**

# 13. Demonstrating the Prototype

The prototype demonstration should make the main user value visible.

The presenter should introduce the user task, demonstrate the core interaction, and connect the demonstration to what the team learned.

The prototype is evidence within the pitch, not a separate activity.

| Demo Stage             | Presenter Action                                            | Audience Understanding                                  |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Set the Context        | Name the user and situation in one sentence.                | Who is using the prototype and why?                     |
| State the Task         | Explain what the user is trying to accomplish.              | What outcome should the prototype support?              |
| Show the Core Flow     | Demonstrate only the essential steps.                       | How does the idea create value?                         |
| Highlight the Change   | Point to the part revised after testing.                    | What did the team improve and why?                      |
| Acknowledge Simulation | Label paper, role-played, or simulated technical behaviour. | Which parts are prototypes rather than working systems? |
| Return to the Story    | End with the testing insight or next step.                  | What did the demonstration help the team learn?         |

## 13.1 Common Demo Risks

| Risk                                            | Better Practice                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Presenter explains every screen or component.   | Show only the path connected to the main user need and test question. |
| Audience cannot see the paper prototype.        | Use larger cards or hold the prototype at chest level.                |
| Prototype fails or materials move out of order. | Prepare labels, number the sequence, and rehearse a recovery line.    |
| Team presents simulated technology as real.     | Clearly explain how the response is being simulated.                  |
| Demonstration takes most of the pitch.          | Set a time limit and control the start and finish.                    |

# 14. Clear and Coordinated Delivery

| Delivery Skill     | Effective Practice                                                  | Avoid                                                      |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Voice              | Use clear volume, natural pace, and emphasis on key words.          | Speaking too quietly, shouting, or rushing.                |
| Body Language      | Maintain steady posture, natural gestures, and audience connection. | Turning completely toward the screen or prototype.         |
| Notes              | Use short prompts for order, evidence, and transitions.             | Reading complete paragraphs.                               |
| Transitions        | Clearly introduce the next idea and speaker.                        | Long pauses, repeated introductions, or interruptions.     |
| Technical Terms    | Explain only the terms needed to understand the user value.         | Using technical language simply to sound advanced.         |
| Prototype Handling | Prepare the order, position, and movement of materials.             | Searching for cards or changing the flow during the pitch. |
| Time Awareness     | Use one agreed signal and complete the closing message.             | Stopping suddenly without completing the story.            |

## 14.1 Confident Does Not Mean Perfect

Confidence means understanding the purpose, evidence, and next idea.

Students may pause, check a short note, or invite a teammate to help. The goal is **clear communication and teamwork**, not memorised performance.

### Recovery Lines

* “Let me restate our main point.”
* “This part is simulated in our prototype.”
* “We do not know that yet; our next test would explore it.”

# 15. Feedback Carousel and Revision

Practice feedback should focus on whether the audience can follow the story and understand the value, evidence, and learning.

Reviewers should use common criteria rather than personal preference.

Presenting teams should listen, record, clarify, and then decide what to change.

| Feedback Criterion      | Reviewer Question                                                       |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Problem Clarity         | Can I explain the problem and affected user after hearing the pitch?    |
| User Evidence           | Did the team show what it learned rather than only state an assumption? |
| Value                   | Is it clear how the idea helps the user?                                |
| Prototype Use           | Did the demonstration make the core experience understandable?          |
| Testing and Improvement | Did the team explain what changed and why?                              |
| Story Flow              | Did each section connect logically to the next?                         |
| Team Delivery           | Were roles, transitions, pace, and timing coordinated?                  |
| Honesty and Next Step   | Did the team explain limits and a realistic next action?                |

## 15.1 Useful Feedback Language

* **Name a Strength:** “The clearest part was ______ because ______.”
* **Identify Confusion:** “I was unsure about ______ when ______.”
* **Request Evidence:** “What evidence supports the statement that ______?”
* **Strengthen Value:** “The user benefit would be clearer if ______.”
* **Improve the Demo:** “During the demonstration, I expected ______.”
* **Improve the Story:** “The connection between ______ and ______ could be clearer.”
* **Protect Time:** “This section could be shortened by ______.”

## 15.2 Selecting Feedback

Teams should:

* use feedback that improves clarity, value, evidence, prototype understanding, or timing;
* prioritise repeated confusion or issues that prevent the audience from following the story;
* avoid adding every suggested feature;
* avoid rewriting the entire project immediately before Demo Day; and
* make at least one meaningful revision and explain why it was made.

# 16. Responding to Audience Questions

Questions help the audience understand the team’s reasoning and help the team identify future learning.

Presenters should listen to the full question, answer directly, and use project evidence whenever possible.

**Honest uncertainty is stronger than inventing an answer.**

| Question Type                | Response Approach                                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Problem or User              | Refer to the Opportunity Map, User Need Card, or user evidence.                    |
| Feature or Technical Detail  | Explain whether the feature exists, is simulated, or belongs to a future version.  |
| Evidence                     | State what the team observed or tested without generalising beyond the evidence.   |
| Safety, Privacy, or Fairness | Explain the boundary considered and any additional checks required.                |
| Why This Idea?               | Connect the solution to the user need, comparison criteria, and value proposition. |
| What Comes Next?             | Identify one realistic test, user group, technical task, or prototype improvement. |
| Unknown Answer               | State what is not known and explain how it could be investigated.                  |

## 16.1 Simple Question-Response Process

**1. LISTEN**
Let the question finish.

**2. PAUSE**
Identify the main point.

**3. ANSWER**
Respond directly using evidence.

**4. INVITE**
Bring in the teammate who owns the relevant detail.

**5. CLOSE**
Finish with the answer or next step.

### Honest Answer

“We have not tested that yet. Our next step would be to test it with [user] by [method].”

# 17. Responsible and Honest Pitching

| Area                   | Required Practice                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Evidence               | Describe the size and type of testing honestly. Do not present a peer activity as proof that all users will respond the same way.  |
| Technology             | Clearly label simulated AI, web, embedded, or cybersecurity behaviour. Do not claim unbuilt functions are operational.             |
| Privacy                | Do not show real passwords, personal details, private messages, addresses, medical information, or account data.                   |
| Safety                 | Do not demonstrate unsafe hardware, real cyber intrusion, harmful experiments, or unsupported medical, legal, or financial advice. |
| Fairness and Inclusion | Avoid stereotypes, exclusionary assumptions, and designs that depend only on colour or difficult language.                         |
| Intellectual Property  | Use original, fictional, or licensed content and acknowledge borrowed assets when required.                                        |
| Impact Claims          | Use terms such as “may,” “aims to,” or “could help” when outcomes have not been measured.                                          |
| Questions              | State what the team does not know and identify a realistic way to learn more.                                                      |

# 18. Demo Day Quality Criteria

| Criterion                | What Strong Evidence Looks Like                                      | Weight |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -----: |
| Problem and User         | Focused problem, clear primary user, and relevant need.              |    20% |
| Value and Idea           | Simple solution linked to a meaningful user benefit.                 |    20% |
| Prototype                | Visible core experience that supports the story.                     |    20% |
| Testing and Improvement  | Specific evidence, learning, and meaningful change.                  |    20% |
| Team Story and Delivery  | Logical structure, shared roles, clear timing, and coordinated demo. |    15% |
| Reflection and Next Step | Honest limits, DECI skill connection, and realistic next action.     |     5% |

These criteria are designed to support feedback and reflection. They should not turn the session into a high-pressure business competition.

**The main goal is to make learning visible.**

# 20. Common Misconceptions and Corrections

| Misconception                                                    | Correction                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| “A pitch is a summary of every worksheet.”                       | A pitch selects the strongest details and connects them into one audience story.                 |
| “The prototype should speak for itself.”                         | The team must explain the user, task, value, evidence, and improvement around the demonstration. |
| “More features make the idea more impressive.”                   | A focused core experience is easier to understand and test.                                      |
| “We should hide negative feedback.”                              | Useful learning often comes from confusion, failed tasks, and changes made after testing.        |
| “Technical language proves the project is advanced.”             | Technical ideas matter when they create understandable user value.                               |
| “One strong speaker should present everything.”                  | Shared participation demonstrates teamwork and builds communication skills.                      |
| “We must accept every peer suggestion.”                          | Teams choose feedback using the user need, evidence, scope, and available time.                  |
| “We should answer every question confidently, even when unsure.” | Honest uncertainty and a clear next test are stronger than invented claims.                      |
| “Demo Day means the business is ready to launch.”                | Level 3 presents a tested project possibility, not a complete startup.                           |

# 21. Key Takeaways

* A strong Demo Day pitch explains the journey from a problem to a tested possibility.
* The audience needs a clear user, need, idea, value, and evidence trail.
* Value explains the useful change for the user, not only the features of the solution.
* Evidence should support important claims and be described honestly.
* The prototype demonstration should show one core user experience and one meaningful improvement.
* The Mini Project One-Pager selects the most important information from Days 1–4.
* Shared speaking and demonstration roles create a coordinated team story.
* A 3–4-minute pitch requires careful selection, timing, and clear transitions.
* Specific peer feedback helps teams improve clarity, evidence, and delivery.
* Audience questions should be answered directly using evidence and honest next-step thinking.
* Level 3 ends with a tested idea and reflection; deeper business modelling belongs to Levels 4 and 5.

How can we clearly show the problem, value, and learning?

Audience and purpose; project evidence trail; entrepreneurial pitch structure; value; evidence-based claims; Mini Project One-Pager; team roles; timing; prototype demonstration; peer feedback; revision; Q&A; and responsible pitching.
