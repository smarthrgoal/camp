# Level 3 – Day 4 Content Document

## Build, Test and Improve

### Prototyping, Testing and Iteration

**Programme:** DECI Summer Camp
**Level:** Level 3 Students
**Day Duration:** 3 hours, including two 15-minute breaks
**Delivery Language:** English
**Learning Approach:** Game-based, hands-on, and evidence-driven
**Daily Output:** Low-Fidelity Prototype + Test and Improvement Log

---

## 1. Day Overview

Day 4 moves teams from a selected project idea to a prototype that others can see, use, and respond to. Students create a low-fidelity prototype that represents the most important part of the user experience. They then test it with peers, collect specific evidence, and use that evidence to improve their prototype.

The goal is not to create a polished final product. Instead, students learn how to test an idea quickly, identify what works and what does not, and make informed improvements before investing more time, materials, or technical effort.

### Essential Question

**How can a simple prototype help us learn quickly?**

### Day Purpose

Students experience the **build–test–learn–improve** cycle and discover that feedback becomes valuable when it is connected to an observation, a question, or a clear user need.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the day, students will be able to:

1. Select an appropriate low-fidelity prototype format for their project idea.
2. Build a prototype that makes one key user experience or assumption visible and testable.
3. Collect specific feedback and observations using agreed testing questions.
4. Revise at least one part of the prototype and explain the evidence behind the change.

# 4. What Is a Prototype?

A prototype is a temporary representation of an idea created to help a team learn something before developing the complete solution.

A prototype can represent a screen, interaction, process, physical object, service experience, or decision flow. It is useful when another person can interact with it and provide information about the part of the idea the team needs to understand.

### Simple Definition

**A prototype is a quick model used to answer a question, not a finished product created to impress people.**

### 4.1 Prototype, Product, and Presentation

| Item                 | Main Purpose                                | Expected Level on Day 4                                                                                           |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Prototype**        | Explore an assumption and collect evidence. | Required: rough, focused, understandable, and testable.                                                           |
| **Finished Product** | Deliver a complete and reliable solution.   | Not required. Teams are not expected to code, manufacture, or launch the full idea.                               |
| **Presentation**     | Explain an idea visually to an audience.    | Not enough by itself. A poster may explain an idea, but a prototype must allow meaningful interaction or testing. |

### 4.2 What Makes a Prototype Useful?

A useful prototype:

* Represents the selected user and user need identified on Days 2 and 3.
* Focuses on one important experience, journey, or assumption.
* Allows another person to interact with it or respond to it.
* Is simple enough to change after testing.
* Avoids unnecessary details that do not help the team learn.
* Is safe, respectful, and honest about any simulated behaviour.

---

# 5. Low-Fidelity Prototyping

Low-fidelity means that the prototype is intentionally simple. It may use basic materials, quick sketches, labels, role-play, or simulated interactions instead of polished designs and fully developed technology.

Low-fidelity prototypes make iteration easier because teams are less attached to the first version and can make changes quickly.

| Characteristic | What It Looks Like                                                                | Why It Helps                                                                                                                 |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fast**       | Built quickly using simple materials.                                             | Teams spend more time learning and less time polishing.                                                                      |
| **Rough**      | Hand-drawn screens, paper controls, labels, or simple models.                     | Testers focus on the idea and user flow rather than visual perfection.                                                       |
| **Focused**    | Shows only the core interaction or user journey.                                  | Teams can test one important assumption clearly.                                                                             |
| **Changeable** | Parts can be moved, rewritten, replaced, or removed.                              | Evidence can lead directly to improvements.                                                                                  |
| **Simulated**  | Unbuilt technology is represented by a person, card, arrow, or scripted response. | Students can safely explore AI, web, cybersecurity, or embedded concepts without claiming that a system is actually working. |

### 5.1 Low Fidelity Does Not Mean Low Thinking

A prototype may look simple, but the decisions behind it should be thoughtful.

Teams should decide:

* What does the user see first?
* What action does the user take?
* What response follows?
* What evidence would show that the idea is clear or useful?

### Quality Principle

**Prototype quality is measured by how well it supports learning—not by how expensive, detailed, or attractive it looks.**

---

# 6. Choosing the Right Prototype Format

The best prototype format depends on the project idea, the main assumption, and the part of the user experience that needs to be tested.

Teams should choose the **simplest format that allows another person to respond meaningfully**.

| Prototype Format            | Best Used For                                                      | Example                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Paper Screens**           | Websites, apps, dashboards, forms, or digital journeys.            | Hand-drawn screens showing how a student finds a school club and registers.                               |
| **Storyboard**              | Experiences that happen over time or across different locations.   | A six-frame story showing how a student discovers, uses, and benefits from the idea.                      |
| **Service Role-Play**       | Human interactions, support services, or team processes.           | One student plays the service provider, another plays the user, and a third observes.                     |
| **Physical Model**          | Devices, objects, spaces, or embedded-system concepts.             | A cardboard device showing inputs, outputs, alerts, and user actions.                                     |
| **Process/System Flow**     | Rules, decisions, information movement, or cybersecurity journeys. | A flow showing login, permissions, safe data handling, and the next user step.                            |
| **Mock Campaign or Visual** | Digital art, awareness, communication, or identity ideas.          | A small set of visual messages tested for clarity and intended action.                                    |
| **Wizard-of-Oz Simulation** | AI or automated behaviour that cannot be built during the camp.    | A team member secretly provides responses while the tester experiences a simulated recommendation system. |

### 6.1 One Idea May Use More Than One Format

A team may combine formats when necessary, such as paper screens with a short role-play.

However, the combination should remain focused. Every additional element should help test the main user experience or assumption.

---

# 7. From Project Idea to Prototype Scope

The Project Idea Card from Day 3 may contain several features. On Day 4, teams must reduce the idea to a **testable slice**.

The prototype should represent the smallest meaningful experience that allows the team to learn something important.

### 7.1 Core Experience

| Question                               | Purpose                                                  | Example                                                                               |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Who is using the prototype?**        | Keep the primary user visible.                           | A new student choosing a school club.                                                 |
| **What is the user trying to do?**     | Define one meaningful task.                              | Compare two clubs and identify the next registration step.                            |
| **What does the user see or receive?** | Identify the essential information, action, or response. | Comparison cards and a clear registration instruction.                                |
| **What should happen next?**           | Make the intended journey visible.                       | The student selects a club and knows where to register.                               |
| **What must we learn?**                | Connect the prototype to the main assumption.            | Can the student understand the comparison and find the next step without explanation? |

### 7.2 Must Show, Could Show, and Not Now

| Priority       | Meaning                                                        | Example for a Club Guide                                               |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Must Show**  | Essential for testing the main experience.                     | Two club options, comparison information, and the registration step.   |
| **Could Show** | Useful addition if time remains.                               | A filter for activity type or meeting day.                             |
| **Not Now**    | Adds complexity but does not help answer the testing question. | Real accounts, notifications, live databases, or full visual branding. |

### Scope Rule

**A smaller prototype that clearly answers one question is stronger than a large prototype that does not clearly test anything.**

---

# 8. Connect the Prototype to the Main Assumption

The main assumption selected on Day 3 gives the prototype its learning target.

Teams should not simply ask whether testers "like" the idea. Instead, they should create a situation that helps reveal whether the assumption is reasonable, unclear, or unsupported.

| Assumption                                            | Prototype Response                                                                          | Evidence to Observe                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Users can compare options without confusion.          | Create two comparison cards and ask the tester to choose one.                               | What information the tester notices, ignores, or asks about.                              |
| Users understand a simulated AI recommendation.       | Show a recommendation with a short explanation and allow the tester to accept or reject it. | Whether the tester understands why the recommendation appeared and whether they trust it. |
| Users notice and understand an embedded-system alert. | Create a device model with a visible alert and ask what action should happen next.          | Whether the alert is noticed and whether the expected action is clear.                    |
| Users can complete a safe login journey.              | Create paper screens showing account access and permission choices.                         | Where the tester hesitates or attempts to share information that should remain private.   |
| A visual message encourages the intended action.      | Show two draft messages and ask the tester what each one asks them to do.                   | Whether the message and intended action are understood correctly.                         |

### 8.1 A Testable Assumption Is Specific

| Weak Assumption               | Stronger Testable Assumption                                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| People will love our project. | A new student can identify one suitable club and the next registration step using the prototype. |
| The design is good.           | Users understand the main button and can predict what will happen after selecting it.            |
| The device will work.         | Users notice the alert and understand which action the alert asks them to take.                  |

# 9. The Build–Test–Learn–Improve Cycle

Iteration means repeating a learning cycle. The first prototype is not expected to be perfect. It creates evidence that helps the team decide what to change next.

### The Four Stages

**1. BUILD**
Represent the core experience quickly.

**2. TEST**
Let another person interact with the prototype while the team observes.

**3. LEARN**
Identify patterns, confusion, questions, and useful reactions.

**4. IMPROVE**
Change one or more elements for a clear, evidence-based reason.

### 9.1 What Each Stage Produces

| Stage       | Team Action                                                                       | Evidence Produced                                                      |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Build**   | Create the first version and prepare the testing task.                            | Prototype Version 1 and one main testing question.                     |
| **Test**    | Invite another team to interact without unnecessary explanation.                  | Actions, comments, questions, hesitations, and task results.           |
| **Learn**   | Separate evidence from personal preference and identify the most important issue. | A short list of findings and remaining uncertainties.                  |
| **Improve** | Keep, change, or remove elements based on findings.                               | Prototype Version 2 and a documented reason for each important change. |

### 9.2 Iteration Is Not Random Editing

Changing colours, adding features, or rewriting everything does not automatically count as improvement.

A meaningful iteration connects the change to evidence and explains how the change may help the user or strengthen the test.

---

# 10. Planning a Useful Test

A useful test gives the tester a realistic task and gives the team a clear reason to observe.

The task should not reveal the expected answer or explain how to use the prototype before the tester has a chance to respond naturally.

### 10.1 Main Testing Question

**Testing Question Template:**

> Can [specific user] use or understand [core experience] well enough to [intended outcome]?

| Project Type                     | Example Testing Question                                                                       |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Paper Website or App**         | Can a new student compare two club options and find the next registration step?                |
| **Physical or Embedded Concept** | Can a classroom user notice the alert and understand which energy-saving action to take?       |
| **AI Simulation**                | Can the user understand why the recommendation was made and decide whether it fits their need? |
| **Cyber-Safety Journey**         | Can the user complete the access flow without sharing unnecessary personal information?        |
| **Visual Campaign**              | Can the viewer identify the message and the requested action within a few seconds?             |

### 10.2 Three Core Testing Prompts

| Prompt                      | What It Reveals                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **What is clear?**          | Parts that communicate the intended meaning or action.              |
| **What is confusing?**      | Labels, sequence, instructions, or expectations that need revision. |
| **What would you do next?** | Whether the user journey and next action are visible.               |

A fourth prompt, **"What is missing?"**, may be used after the tester completes the task. It should not become an invitation to add every possible feature.

---

# 11. Roles During Prototype Testing

Testing works best when team roles are clearly defined.

The creator team should avoid teaching, correcting, or defending the prototype while the tester is using it. Silence can reveal where the experience needs improvement.

| Role                   | Main Responsibility                                                                | Avoid                                                            |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Facilitator**        | Give the task, remind the tester to think aloud, and ask the agreed prompts.       | Explaining the correct path or leading the tester to an answer.  |
| **Observer**           | Record actions, comments, questions, hesitations, and task results.                | Recording personal judgement instead of visible evidence.        |
| **Prototype Operator** | Move paper screens or simulate technical behaviour when required.                  | Changing the response to make the idea appear successful.        |
| **Tester**             | Interact naturally, explain expectations, and describe what is clear or confusing. | Giving only positive feedback to protect the creators' feelings. |

### 11.1 Observe Without Defending

| Creator Reaction                          | Better Testing Behaviour                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "No, you should click here."              | Record where the tester clicked and ask, "What did you expect to happen?"             |
| "You did not understand our idea."        | Ask which part was unclear and note what explanation the prototype failed to provide. |
| "But everyone else liked it."             | Focus on the current evidence and look for patterns across testers.                   |
| "We can add every feature you suggested." | Ask whether the suggestion supports the user need and main assumption.                |

### Testing Mindset

**Confusion is not failure. It is information that the team could not discover by discussing the idea alone.**

---

# 12. Evidence, Feedback, and Opinion

Teams should collect different types of information and avoid treating every comment as equally important.

Visible behaviour and repeated patterns often provide stronger evidence than a single personal preference.

| Information Type | Example                                                             | How to Use It                                                                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Observation**  | The tester looked at three labels before selecting the main action. | Review whether the labels and visual order are clear.                                    |
| **Direct Quote** | "I thought this button would show more details."                    | Compare the expected action with the intended action.                                    |
| **Task Result**  | The tester completed the journey but missed the safety warning.     | Keep the successful path and improve the warning.                                        |
| **Question**     | "Where does this information come from?"                            | Identify missing trust, source, or explanation information.                              |
| **Preference**   | "I prefer green instead of blue."                                   | Use only when visual preference affects meaning, accessibility, or the testing question. |
| **Suggestion**   | "Add a chat feature."                                               | Check whether it supports the user need, scope, and evidence before adopting it.         |

### 12.1 Specific Feedback

| Weak Feedback        | Stronger Feedback                                                                           |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "It is good."        | "The comparison cards were clear, but I did not know which action completed registration."  |
| "I do not like it."  | "I expected the alert to explain what action to take, but it only showed a warning symbol." |
| "Add more features." | "I needed a way to compare meeting times before choosing an option."                        |

---

# 13. Deciding What to Change

After testing, teams review the evidence and decide what to **keep, change, remove, or investigate later**.

The goal is not to follow every suggestion. The goal is to make the prototype more useful, clearer, and better aligned with the selected user need.

| Decision              | Use When                                                                                | Example                                                                    |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Keep**              | Evidence shows that the element is clear and supports the user journey.                 | Testers understood the comparison cards and used them successfully.        |
| **Change**            | The element is important but confusing, incomplete, or difficult to use.                | Rewrite the registration action and place it directly after the selection. |
| **Remove**            | The element distracts from the main task, creates risk, or adds unnecessary complexity. | Remove a simulated chat feature that did not support the testing question. |
| **Investigate Later** | The issue matters but cannot be answered through the current test.                      | Explore how real club information would be updated in a future version.    |

### 13.1 Prioritising Changes

Prioritise changes according to the following order:

1. **Blocks the User** – The user cannot understand, continue, or complete the core task.
2. **Creates Risk** – The experience may create privacy, cybersecurity, physical safety, fairness, or trust concerns.
3. **Weakens Value** – The prototype does not make the promised benefit visible.
4. **Improves Clarity** – The journey works, but wording, order, or signals could be clearer.
5. **Visual Preference** – The suggestion affects appearance but not the current learning question.

### 13.2 Explain the Reason

**Change Statement Template:**

> We changed [part] because [evidence]. This should help the user [expected improvement].

# 14. Test and Improvement Log: Content Standard

The Test and Improvement Log captures the learning behind the revised prototype.

It becomes evidence for the Day 5 Demo Day story and ensures that the final presentation shows not only the finished version, but also what the team learned and how the idea changed.

| Log Field                         | What the Team Records                                               | Quality Check                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Prototype Version**             | A short description or image reference for Version 1 and Version 2. | Can another person identify what changed?                         |
| **Main Assumption**               | The important belief selected on Day 3.                             | Is it specific enough to explore through the prototype?           |
| **Testing Question**              | The question the test was designed to answer.                       | Does it focus on one user and one core experience?                |
| **Testing Task**                  | What the tester was asked to do.                                    | Is the task realistic without revealing the answer?               |
| **What We Observed**              | Actions, comments, questions, hesitations, and task results.        | Does the record describe evidence rather than personal judgement? |
| **What Was Clear**                | Parts that worked as intended.                                      | Is the conclusion supported by the test?                          |
| **What Was Confusing or Missing** | Important barriers, misunderstandings, or gaps.                     | Does it focus on the core user journey?                           |
| **Decision**                      | What the team will keep, change, remove, or investigate later.      | Is the decision connected to evidence?                            |
| **Change Made**                   | The revision completed after testing.                               | Is at least one meaningful change visible?                        |
| **Reason for the Change**         | Why the revision was selected.                                      | Does the reason explain how the change may help the user?         |
| **Remaining Uncertainty**         | What the team still does not know.                                  | Is the team honest about its limitations and next questions?      |

### 14.1 Minimum Day 4 Evidence

By the end of Day 4, each team should have:

* Prototype Version 1 representing the core user experience.
* One main testing question and a realistic testing task.
* At least one peer-testing interaction.
* Specific observations and feedback.
* At least one meaningful revision.
* A completed explanation of what changed and why.
* One remaining uncertainty or next question.

# 15. Responsible and Safe Prototyping

Students may develop ideas related to web development, AI, embedded systems, digital art, or cybersecurity.

All unbuilt behaviour must be clearly labelled as simulated, and all testing must remain safe and respectful.

| Area                   | Required Boundary                                                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Personal Data**      | Do not collect real passwords, account details, medical information, precise addresses, or other sensitive data.                                        |
| **Cybersecurity**      | Do not attempt real account access, scanning, bypassing controls, or harmful security experiments. Use fictional scenarios and paper-based flows.       |
| **AI**                 | Do not claim that the prototype uses a real AI model when responses are simulated. Explain that a person or predefined rule is generating the response. |
| **Embedded Systems**   | Use cardboard, paper, safe craft materials, and simulated inputs or outputs. Do not use mains electricity, dangerous tools, or unsafe moving parts.     |
| **Images and Content** | Use original, fictional, or appropriately licensed materials. Avoid harmful stereotypes and unsupported claims.                                         |
| **Accessibility**      | Use readable labels, clear contrast, understandable language, and more than colour alone to communicate important information.                          |
| **Testing Respect**    | Ask permission, focus feedback on the work, and do not embarrass or pressure testers.                                                                   |

# 17. Common Misconceptions and Corrections

| Misconception                                                | Correction                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "A prototype must work like a finished product."             | A prototype only needs to represent the part required for learning.                                    |
| "A beautiful prototype is automatically a strong prototype." | Visual polish matters only when it supports clarity or the testing question.                           |
| "We should show every planned feature."                      | The prototype should focus on the smallest meaningful experience.                                      |
| "We should explain the prototype before the tester uses it." | Unnecessary explanation can hide confusion that the team needs to discover.                            |
| "If a tester is confused, the tester made a mistake."        | Confusion is evidence about wording, order, signals, or expectations.                                  |
| "All feedback must be accepted."                             | Teams should use evidence, user needs, scope, safety, and patterns to decide what to adopt.            |
| "A suggestion is the same as evidence."                      | A suggestion is one possibility. Evidence includes observed behaviour, quotes, and task results.       |
| "Adding features is the main way to improve."                | Improvement may involve simplifying, reordering, relabelling, or removing elements.                    |
| "Simulated technology is dishonest."                         | Simulation is appropriate when it is clearly labelled and used safely to test an experience.           |
| "The second version is final."                               | Version 2 represents the team's current learning. Real projects require further testing and iteration. |

---

# 18. Key Takeaways

* A prototype is a **learning tool**, not a finished product.
* Low-fidelity prototypes are fast, focused, changeable, and honest about simulated behaviour.
* The best prototype format is the simplest one that can test the main assumption.
* Teams should represent one core user experience rather than every possible feature.
* Testing should use a realistic task and a clear question.
* Creators should observe and record evidence without defending the prototype.
* Specific feedback explains what is clear, confusing, missing, or expected next.
* Iteration means keeping, changing, or removing elements for an evidence-based reason.
* The Test and Improvement Log makes the team's learning visible for Demo Day.

---

# 19. Reflection Questions

1. What did the prototype reveal that discussion alone did not?
2. Which piece of evidence had the greatest effect on our revision?
3. What important question about the idea still remains unanswered?
